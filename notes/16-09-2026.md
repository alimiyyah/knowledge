# Islamic Family Law — Nasab, Hadanah, and Related Rules
**Date:** 2026-09-17
**Source transcript:** 5M Class - 2026-09-09

---

## Summary
This session covered the Islamic legal framework for establishing paternity (nasab) across a range of marital and post-marital scenarios, including different types of divorce, widowhood, and slave women. The class then moved into child custody (hadanah), detailing the hierarchy of custodians, conditions for loss of custody, age thresholds for transfer, and rules around relocation. Modern issues such as DNA evidence were also addressed.

---

## Key Concepts
- **Nasab (Paternity/Lineage)** — the legal establishment of a child's parentage under Islamic law, determining who is recognised as the father and what rights and obligations flow from that recognition
- **Firaash (Marital Bed Presumption)** — when a valid marriage exists, the husband is presumed the father of any child born within it; the primary and default basis for establishing nasab
- **Ulooq (Conception)** — the moment of conception; Islamic jurisprudence determines paternity by examining whether conception could plausibly have occurred during a valid marriage or waiting period
- **'Iddah (Waiting Period)** — the mandatory period a woman must observe after divorce or the death of her husband before remarrying; its duration and type directly affect how long nasab can be established after the marriage ends
- **Hadanah (Custody)** — the right and responsibility to physically care for and raise a child after the separation of parents; Islamic law establishes a detailed hierarchy of who holds this right
- **Umm al-Walad** — a slave woman who gives birth to her master's child; this status grants her significant legal protections, including that she cannot be sold and becomes free upon her master's death

## Detailed Notes

### 1. Foundational Framework for Establishing Nasab

The primary basis for nasab is **firaash**: if a valid marriage exists and a child is born within it, the husband is presumed the father without any further proof required. Islamic law prioritises establishing nasab wherever possible, because denying it carries the implication of accusing the mother of adultery (zina), which requires an extraordinarily high evidentiary standard — four witnesses.

Two gestation limits govern nasab:

- **Minimum gestation period: six lunar months.** A child born fewer than six months after the marriage contract was concluded cannot be attributed to the husband, because conception must have occurred before the marriage.
- **Maximum gestation period** (school-dependent):
  - **Hanafi school** (Imams Abu Hanifah and Muhammad): **two years**
  - **Imam al-Shafi'i:** four years
  - **Imam Malik:** five years

The Hanafi two-year maximum is based on normative cases; scholars acknowledged that modern medicine has documented rare pregnancies beyond two years, but a clear legal limit must be set.

### 2. Nasab Across Different Divorce and Separation Scenarios

#### Scenario 1 — Revocable Divorce (Talaq Raj'i)

- If the wife is divorced revocably and gives birth **within two years** of the divorce, nasab is established. Conception before the divorce is possible, and conception during the 'iddah via ruju' (return) is also possible — since doubt exists, nasab is established as a precaution (*ihtiyatan*).
- If she gives birth **after two years**, nasab is not established automatically. However, if the husband **claims** the child, this is treated as an **implicit ruju'** — he is acknowledging he returned to the marriage (even without formal declaration) and the child is his. This is a distinctive Hanafi mechanism to protect nasab without requiring explicit ruju' formalities.

#### Scenario 2 — Irrevocable Divorce (Talaq Ba'in)

- After a ba'in divorce, the husband has no right of return. If the child is born **within two years**, nasab is still established because conception before the divorce is plausible.
- If born **after two years**, nasab is not established unless the husband explicitly claims the child. At that point, it is certain that conception occurred after the divorce when intercourse was unlawful — unless he claims intercourse occurred by genuine mistake (*shubha*), which provides a valid legal basis. (Islamic law treats intercourse by genuine mistake as legally equivalent to valid intercourse for the purpose of nasab, because the legal consequences of nasab — child's rights, inheritance — should not be denied due to an honest mistake. This does not render the intercourse halal; it merely activates the nasab and mahr consequences.)

#### Scenario 3 — Widow ('Iddah of Death)

- If the husband dies and the wife gives birth **within two years** of his death, nasab is established.
- If she gives birth **after the 'iddah of death is complete** (four months and ten days) but **within six months of the 'iddah ending**, **Imam al-Zufar** holds nasab is not established — the Sharia has legally ended her 'iddah through months, unlike a pregnant woman whose 'iddah ends with delivery.
- **Mainstream position:** nasab is still established up to two years from the death, because pregnancy remains a possible alternative basis for the 'iddah.

#### Scenario 4 — Minor (Pre-Pubescent) Divorced Wife

A minor girl's 'iddah is calculated in months (three months), not by menstrual cycles.

- **Imam Abu Hanifah:** Nasab is only established if the child is born **within nine months** of the divorce. For a pre-pubescent girl, pregnancy before puberty is considered legally impossible (the default is *'adam al-haml*). Once the three-month 'iddah passes — ended by the Sharia's own ruling, not her declaration — there is no basis to assume she was pregnant at the time of divorce. The nine-month window covers the maximum time from the end of 'iddah backward to the divorce.
- **Imam Abu Yusuf:** Extends the period to **two years**, because she has not explicitly declared her 'iddah complete, so pregnancy remains a theoretical possibility.
- **Raj'i divorce, minor wife (Abu Yusuf):** Up to **27 months** — three months of 'iddah (during which intercourse via ruju' could occur at the very end) plus two years' maximum gestation.
- **If the minor herself claims pregnancy** during the 'iddah, both major and minor cases are treated equally, because her claim constitutes an implicit acknowledgment of puberty.

### 3. Child Born Fewer Than Six Months After the Marriage Contract

- Nasab is **not** established by default, because conception certainly preceded the marriage.
- **Exception:** If the **husband himself claims** the child (*da'wa*), nasab is established from his claim. The rationale: he has voluntarily taken on this obligation, and there is a conceivable basis (e.g., intercourse by shubha before the marriage).
- Once the husband claims the child, the **mahr (dower) is also confirmed**, because a legal intercourse is implied.

---

### 4. Testimony Required to Prove Birth (Wiladat)

- **Imam Abu Hanifah:** Birth must be testified to by **two men, or one man and two women** (standard full testimony requirement).
  - **Exception 1:** If pregnancy was already apparent and widely known, testimony is not required — the visible pregnancy itself serves as evidence.
  - **Exception 2:** If the husband had previously acknowledged the pregnancy, testimony for the birth is not required.
- **Imam Abu Yusuf and Imam Muhammad:** The testimony of **one woman** (e.g., a midwife or female doctor) is sufficient to establish birth for the purposes of nasab, in matters that men cannot typically witness.

#### Contingent Talaq and the One-Woman Testimony

If a husband says "if you give birth, you are divorced":
- **Imam Abu Hanifah:** If only one woman testifies to the birth, the **divorce does not take effect**. The one-woman testimony for birth is accepted as a necessity (*darurah*) in its own domain — because men cannot be present at birth — but this necessity does not extend beyond its domain. Divorce is a separate legal act with its own proof requirements; since it is separable from the birth, full proof is required.
- **Imam Abu Yusuf and Imam Muhammad:** Since the testimony is accepted for the birth, it should be accepted for whatever is legally built upon that birth — so the **divorce does take effect**.

---

### 5. Heirs Confirming Birth After the Husband's Death

- When a husband dies and the **heirs acknowledge** the birth without any witness, nasab is established in all scholars' opinions. This is purely the heirs' own right, and their acknowledgment is binding on themselves.
- Regarding nasab **vis-à-vis third parties** (e.g., does the child become a sibling to others?): if the heirs are legally qualified witnesses, their acknowledgment can constitute testimony and establish nasab for third parties as well.
- Whether the heirs must use the formal language of "testimony" (*lafzat al-shahada*) is disputed: one view holds it is required; another holds it is not necessary, since the nasab in relation to third parties is subsidiary to its establishment in relation to the heirs themselves.

### 6. Slave Woman (Amah) and Nasab

If a man marries a slave woman, then divorces her, then purchases her:

- **Child born fewer than six months from the date of purchase:** the child is attributed to him as a child of the 'iddah from the marriage — conception preceded the purchase.
- **Child born six months or more after the purchase:** the child is not automatically his; he must claim it (*da'wa*), because conception plausibly occurred after the purchase, making it a child of the slave relationship rather than the marriage.
- **If the divorce was two or three ba'in talaqs** (permanently forbidden until she marries another man): nasab can be established up to **two years from the divorce**, not from the purchase, because the legal relationship of the marriage is the relevant reference point.

Once a slave woman is established as **Umm al-Walad**, she gains protected status: she **cannot be sold** and **becomes free upon her master's death**. The claim of being Umm al-Walad can be established through the woman's own testimony or through a single female witness.

---

### 7. DNA and Medical Evidence in Modern Context

- Medical evidence (DNA, ultrasound gestational dating) is admissible as **supporting or corroborating evidence** at the *ta'zir* level, particularly when the state is prosecuting a case.
- It **cannot replace** the foundational Quranic evidentiary standard: four witnesses for zina, or the established firaash presumption for nasab.
- If a husband wishes to deny his child using medical evidence, he must still go through the formal process of **li'an** (mutual oath-swearing for denial of paternity); medical evidence alone does not override firaash.
- Medical evidence may be used to **protect a woman from a false accusation**, or by the state in a tazir (discretionary punishment) proceeding.

### 8. Child Custody (Hadanah) — Hierarchy and Rules

#### Primary Right: The Mother

Upon separation of spouses, the **mother has the primary right** to custody of young children.

- **Basis:** A hadith in which a woman came to the Prophet ﷺ saying her womb carried the child, her lap sheltered him, and her breast fed him — the Prophet ﷺ affirmed her right as long as she does not remarry.
- **Rationale:** The mother is more compassionate (*ashfaq*) and more capable of raising young children; it is in the child's best interest to be with her.
- Sayyiduna Abu Bakr al-Siddiq (رضي الله عنه) reportedly said to Sayyiduna Umar (رضي الله عنه) regarding a child: *"Her saliva is better for him than honey and clarified butter with you, O Umar"* — illustrating the primacy of maternal care.
- **Financial responsibility (nafaqah) remains on the father** even when the mother has custody; the mother cannot be compelled to provide financial support.

#### Loss of Custody — Remarriage

- The mother **loses her right to custody** if she remarries a man who is a **non-mahram** to the child.
- If she marries a **mahram of the child** (e.g., the child's paternal uncle), she **does not lose custody**.
- If she later **divorces** the new husband, her right to custody **returns**.

#### Hierarchy of Custodians After the Mother

Custody passes first through female relatives on the maternal side, then more broadly:

1. Maternal grandmother (*nani*), then further up the maternal line
2. Paternal grandmother (*dadi*)
3. Full sisters (*ukht shaqiqa*)
4. Maternal half-sisters
5. Paternal half-sisters
6. Maternal aunts (*khala*) — full, then maternal half, then paternal half
7. Paternal aunts (*phuphi*) — in similar order

The rationale for prioritising maternal relatives: *shafaqah* (compassion) is stronger on the mother's side. The Quran's mention of Prophet Yusuf (عليه السلام) calling his maternal aunt "mother" (*khala*) supports the elevated status of the khala.

#### When Custody Passes to Male Relatives (Asaba)

When no suitable female relative is available, custody passes to **male mahram relatives of the asaba (agnatic) line**:

Father → Paternal grandfather → Full brother → Paternal half-brother → Paternal uncle, etc.

- A male relative who is **not an asaba** (e.g., a maternal uncle, *khalu*) does **not** receive custody. The khalu is a mahram but not an asaba; custody among male relatives is restricted to those who are both mahram and asaba. There is also concern about fitna with a non-asaba male.

#### Age at Which Custody Transfers to the Father

- **Boys:** Custody with the mother (or female relatives) continues until the boy can manage his basic needs independently — eating, dressing, using the bathroom without assistance. Generally around **7 years of age**.
- **Girls:** Custody with the mother continues until **puberty (*bulugh*)** — a girl needs her mother to teach her the etiquette, manners, and knowledge specific to women.

After these ages, the child moves to the **father (or male asaba)**, who is better positioned to teach worldly and religious skills, social conduct, and to protect from fitna. Some scholars note the child's preference is considered once the child reaches the relevant age, particularly if mature enough to make an informed decision.

#### Custody and Non-Muslim Mothers

- If the mother is non-Muslim, her right to custody is limited to the period before the child is at risk of being influenced away from Islam.
- As soon as there is a genuine fear (*ehtimal*) that the non-Muslim mother will lead the child away from the deen, the Muslim Qadi (judge) will transfer custody.
- For girls specifically: since a girl remains with the mother until puberty, the risk of religious indoctrination is higher and the court will be more vigilant.

#### Custody and Relocation

- A divorced mother may **not relocate the child to a different city** without the father's consent, as this causes harm to the father's right of access.
- **Exception:** She may relocate to her **original hometown (*watan*)**, provided the marriage originally took place in that city — because the husband implicitly accepted she might return there. (Supporting hadith: *"Whoever settles in a town, he is considered one of its people."*)
- If the two cities are **close enough that the father can visit daily and return home at night**, relocation is permissible.
- **Village to city:** Permitted — benefits the child's upbringing and social development.
- **City to village:** Not permitted — harms the child.
- The same rules apply to relocation between two towns (*qaryatayn*).

#### Father's Right of Access

Even when the mother has custody, the **father has the right to see his child daily**. "Milna" (meeting/overnight stay) means the child sleeps at the father's home; the counting of custody days is based on **overnight stays**, not daytime visits.

## Definitions / Terminology

- **Nasab:** The legal establishment of a child's parentage under Islamic law; determines who is recognised as the father and what rights and obligations flow from that recognition
- **Firaash:** The legal concept of a valid marital bed; when a valid marriage exists, the husband is presumed to be the father of any child
