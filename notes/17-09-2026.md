# Islamic Family Law — Nasab, Hadanah, and Related Rules
**Date:** 2026-09-09
**Source transcript:** 5M Class - 2026-09-09

## Summary
This class covered the Islamic legal framework for establishing paternity (nasab) under Hanafi jurisprudence, including the rules governing different divorce and death scenarios, testimony requirements for establishing birth, and the treatment of slave women. The second half addressed child custody (hadanah), including the hierarchy of custodial rights, conditions for losing custody, age of transfer, and relocation rules. Modern applications of DNA evidence were also briefly discussed.

---

## Key Concepts
- **Nasab (Lineage/Paternity)** — the legal establishment of a child's parentage under Islamic law, determining who is recognized as the father and what rights and obligations flow from that recognition
- **Firaash (Marital Bed Presumption)** — when a valid marriage exists, the husband is presumed to be the father of any child born within it; this is the primary basis for establishing nasab
- **Ulooq (Conception)** — the moment of conception; jurisprudence determines paternity by examining whether conception could plausibly have occurred during a valid marriage or 'iddah
- **'Iddah (Waiting Period)** — the mandatory waiting period a woman must observe after divorce or the death of her husband; its duration and type directly affect how long nasab can be established after the marriage ends
- **Hadanah (Custody)** — the right and responsibility to physically care for and raise a child after parental separation; Islamic law establishes a detailed hierarchy of who holds this right
- **Umm al-Walad** — a slave woman who gives birth to her master's child; this status grants her significant legal protections including freedom upon the master's death

## Detailed Notes

### 1. The Foundation of Nasab: Firaash and Gestation Periods

The primary basis for nasab is **firaash** — if a valid marriage exists and a child is born within it, the husband is presumed the father without any further proof required. Islamic law prioritises establishing nasab wherever possible, because denying it carries the implicit accusation that the mother committed adultery (zina), which requires an extremely high evidentiary standard of four witnesses.

**Minimum gestation period:** Six lunar months. A child born fewer than six months after the marriage contract cannot be attributed to the husband, because conception must have occurred before the marriage was contracted.

**Maximum gestation period (school differences):**
- Hanafi school (Imams Abu Hanifah and Muhammad): **two years**
- Imam al-Shafi'i: four years
- Imam Malik: five years

### 2. Nasab Across Different Divorce and Death Scenarios

#### Scenario 1 — Revocable Divorce (Talaq Raj'i)
- If the wife is divorced revocably and gives birth **within two years** of the divorce, nasab is established — conception before the divorce is plausible, and conception during 'iddah via ruju' (return) is also possible. Because doubt exists, nasab is established as a precaution (ihtiyatan).
- If she gives birth **after two years**, nasab is not established automatically. However, if the husband claims the child, this is treated as an implicit ruju' — he is acknowledging he returned to the marriage even without a formal declaration. This is a uniquely Hanafi mechanism to protect nasab without requiring explicit ruju' formalities.

#### Scenario 2 — Irrevocable Divorce (Talaq Ba'in)
- After a ba'in divorce, the husband has no right of return. If the child is born **within two years**, nasab is still established because conception before the divorce remains plausible.
- If born **after two years**, nasab is not established unless the husband explicitly claims the child. In this case, his claim implies intercourse by mistake (shubha), which provides a valid legal basis — Islamic law treats intercourse by genuine mistake as legally equivalent to valid intercourse for purposes of nasab. The child's rights (inheritance, etc.) should not be denied due to an honest error, though the intercourse itself is not rendered halal.

#### Scenario 3 — Widow ('Iddah of Death)
- If the husband dies and the wife gives birth **within two years** of his death, nasab is established.
- **Imam al-Zufar's dissent:** If she gives birth after the 'iddah of death is complete (four months and ten days) but within six months of the 'iddah ending, al-Zufar holds nasab is not established, because the Sharia has legally ended her 'iddah through the months — unlike a pregnant woman whose 'iddah ends only with delivery.
- **Mainstream position:** Nasab is still established up to two years from the death, because pregnancy is an alternative and valid basis for the 'iddah, meaning the months-based 'iddah does not definitively close the door.

#### Scenario 4 — Minor (Pre-Pubescent) Divorced Wife
A minor girl's 'iddah is calculated in months (three months), not menstrual cycles.

- **Imam Abu Hanifah:** Nasab is only established if the child is born **within nine months** of the divorce. The reasoning: a pre-pubescent girl is presumed legally incapable of pregnancy (the default is 'adam al-haml). Once the three-month 'iddah passes — ended by the Sharia's own ruling through months, not by her own declaration — there is no basis to assume she was pregnant at the time of the divorce. The nine-month window covers the maximum time from the end of the 'iddah backward to the divorce.
- **Imam Abu Yusuf:** Extends the period to **two years**, treating her analogously to an adult woman in this respect: she has not explicitly declared her 'iddah complete, so pregnancy remains a theoretical open possibility.
- **Abu Yusuf for raj'i divorce of a minor:** Calculates up to **27 months** — three months of 'iddah (during which intercourse via ruju' could theoretically occur at the very end) plus two years maximum gestation.
- **Special case:** If the minor herself claims pregnancy during the 'iddah, both major and minor cases are treated equally, because her claim constitutes an implicit acknowledgment of puberty.

### 3. Child Born Fewer Than Six Months After the Marriage Contract

- Nasab is **not** established by default, because conception certainly preceded the marriage.
- **Exception:** If the husband himself claims the child (da'wa), nasab is established from his claim. The rationale is that he has voluntarily taken on this obligation and there is a conceivable basis — e.g., intercourse by shubha before the marriage.
- Once the husband claims the child, **mahr (dower) is also confirmed**, because a legally recognised intercourse is implied.

---

### 4. Testimony Required to Prove Birth (Wiladat)

**Imam Abu Hanifah:** Birth must be testified to by two men, or one man and two women — the standard full testimony requirement.
- **Exception 1:** If the pregnancy was already apparent and widely known, testimony is not required; the visible pregnancy itself serves as evidence.
- **Exception 2:** If the husband had previously acknowledged the pregnancy, testimony is not required for the birth.

**Imam Abu Yusuf and Imam Muhammad:** The testimony of one woman (e.g., a midwife or female doctor) is sufficient to establish birth for the purposes of nasab. Their reasoning: birth is a matter men cannot typically witness, so the Sharia accommodates necessity (darurah) here.

**The contingent talaq case — a subtle distinction (Imam Abu Hanifah):**
If a husband says "if you give birth, you are divorced," and only one woman testifies to the birth:
- **Abu Hanifah:** The divorce does **not** take effect. The one-woman testimony is accepted as a necessity within its own domain (establishing the birth), but this necessity does not automatically extend to trigger legal consequences outside of it. Divorce is a separate legal act with its own proof requirements, and because the divorce is separable from the birth itself, full proof is required to activate it.
- **Abu Yusuf and Muhammad:** The divorce **does** take effect — since the testimony is accepted for the birth, it should be accepted for whatever is legally built upon that birth.

### 5. Heirs Confirming the Birth of a Deceased Husband's Child

- When a husband dies and the heirs acknowledge the birth without any formal witness, nasab is established in **all scholars' opinions** — this is purely the heirs' own right, and their acknowledgment is binding upon themselves.
- Regarding nasab vis-à-vis third parties (e.g., does the child become a sibling to others): if the heirs are legally qualified witnesses, their acknowledgment can constitute testimony and establish nasab for third parties as well.
- Whether the heirs must use the formal language of testimony (lafzat al-shahada) is disputed: one view holds it is required; another holds it is not necessary, since the nasab in relation to third parties is subsidiary to its establishment in relation to the heirs themselves.

### 6. Slave Woman (Amah) — Nasab and Umm al-Walad

If a man marries a slave woman, divorces her, then later purchases her:
- If she gives birth **fewer than six months** from the date of purchase → the child is attributed to him (conceived during the marriage 'iddah; conception preceded the purchase).
- If she gives birth **six months or more** after the purchase → the child is not automatically his; he must make a da'wa (claim), because conception plausibly occurred after the purchase, making it a child of the slave relationship rather than the marriage.
- If the divorce was **two or three talaq ba'in** (permanently forbidden until she marries another): nasab can be established up to **two years from the divorce**, not from the date of purchase, because the marriage is the legally relevant reference point.

**Umm al-Walad status:** Once a slave woman is established as the mother of her master's child, she gains protected status — she cannot be sold, and she becomes free upon her master's death. The claim of being Umm al-Walad can be established through the woman's own testimony or through a single female witness.

### 7. DNA and Medical Evidence in the Modern Context

- Medical evidence (DNA, ultrasound gestational dating) is admissible as **supporting or corroborating evidence** at the ta'zir level, particularly in state-prosecuted cases, but cannot replace the foundational Quranic evidentiary standards (four witnesses for zina; firaash presumption for nasab).
- If a husband wishes to deny paternity using medical evidence, he must still go through the formal process of **li'an** (mutual oath-swearing for denial of paternity); medical evidence alone does not override the firaash presumption.
- Medical evidence may be used to protect a woman from a false accusation, or by the state in a discretionary (tazir) proceeding.
- Modern medicine has documented rare cases of pregnancy lasting beyond two years, but the Hanafi school sets two years as the legal maximum based on normative cases and the principle that a clear legal limit must be established.

### 8. Child Custody (Hadanah) — Framework and Hierarchy

#### The Mother's Primary Right
Upon separation of spouses, the mother has the **primary right** to custody of young children.

**Basis:** A hadith in which a woman came to the Prophet ﷺ saying her womb carried the child, her lap sheltered him, and her breast fed him — the Prophet ﷺ affirmed her right as long as she does not remarry.

**Rationale:** The mother is more compassionate (ashfaq) and more capable of raising young children; it is in the child's best interest to be with her. Sayyiduna Abu Bakr al-Siddiq (رضي الله عنه) said to Sayyiduna Umar (رضي الله عنه) regarding a child: *"Her saliva is better for him than honey and clarified butter with you, O Umar"* — illustrating the primacy of maternal care.

**Financial responsibility:** Nafaqah (financial maintenance) remains on the **father** even when the mother has custody; the mother cannot be compelled to provide financial support.

#### Loss of the Mother's Custody Right
- The mother **loses** her right to custody if she remarries a man who is a **non-mahram** to the child.
- If she marries a **mahram** of the child (e.g., the child's paternal uncle), she does **not** lose custody.
- If she subsequently divorces the new husband, her right to custody **returns**.

#### Hierarchy of Custodians After the Mother
When the mother is unavailable or has lost her right, custody passes in order:

**Female relatives (prioritised by maternal side first):**
1. Maternal grandmother (nani), then further up the maternal line
2. Paternal grandmother (dadi)
3. Full sisters
4. Maternal half-sisters
5. Paternal half-sisters
6. Maternal aunts (khala) — full, then maternal half, then paternal half
7. Paternal aunts (phuphi) — in similar order

*Rationale for prioritising maternal relatives:* Compassion (shafaqah) is stronger on the mother's side. The Quran's reference to Prophet Yusuf (عليه السلام) calling his maternal aunt "mother" (khala) further supports the elevated status of the khala.

**When custody passes to male relatives (Asaba):**
When no suitable female relative is available, custody passes to **mahram asaba** — those who are both agnatic (related through the male line) and mahram:
1. Father
2. Paternal grandfather
3. Full brother
4. Paternal half-brother
5. Paternal uncle, and so on

*Note:* A male relative who is not an asaba (e.g., a maternal uncle / khalu) does **not** receive custody. Although the khalu is a mahram, he is not an asaba, and there is additional concern about fitna. The asaba condition and mahram condition must both be met for a male to receive custody.

---

### 9. Age at Which Custody Transfers to the Father

- **Boys:** Custody with the mother (or female relatives) continues until the boy can manage his basic needs independently — eating, dressing, and using the bathroom without assistance. This is generally around **7 years of age**.
- **Girls:** Custody with the mother continues until **puberty (bulugh)**. The rationale is that a girl needs her mother to teach her the etiquette, manners, and knowledge specific to women.

After these ages, the child moves to the father (or male asaba), who is better positioned to teach the child worldly and religious skills, social conduct, and to provide protection from fitna. The child's own preference may also be considered once the child is mature enough to make an informed decision.

---

### 10. Custody and Non-Muslim Mothers

- If the mother is non-Muslim, her right to custody is **limited** to the period before the child is at risk of being influenced away from Islam.
- As soon as there is a genuine fear (ehtimal) that the non-Muslim mother will lead the child away from the deen, the Muslim Qadi (judge) will transfer custody.
- For girls specifically: since a girl remains with the mother until puberty, the risk of religious indoctrination is higher and the court will be more vigilant.

---

### 11. Custody and Relocation

- A divorced mother **may not** relocate the child to a different city without the father's consent, as this harms the father's right of access to his child.
- **Exception — watan (hometown):** She may relocate to her original hometown, provided the marriage originally took place in that city — because the husband implicitly accepted she might return there. The cited hadith: *"Whoever settles in a town, he is considered one of its people."*
- If the two cities are close enough that the father can visit the child and return home the same day, relocation is permissible.
- **Village to city:** Permitted, even if the city is not her watan, because it benefits the child's upbringing and social development.
- **City to village:** Not permitted, as it harms the child.
- The same principles apply to relocation between two towns (qaryatayn).

---

### 12. Father's Right of Access

Even when the mother has custody, the father has the right to see his child. **"Milna" (meeting/overnight stay)** is defined as the child being in the father's care overnight — i.e., the child sleeps at the father's home. The counting of custody time is based on **overnight stays**, not daytime visits.
